## Working Papers

### Climate, Pollution and Endogenous Retirement

**Abstract:** Environmental degradation alters labor supply through mortality risk, productivity loss, and the disutility of working under adverse conditions. This paper develops a dynamic life-cycle framework in continuous time in which retirement arises endogenously, shaped by agents’ choices over health effort, environmental abatement, and labor supply. A distinct mechanism is introduced: workers experience direct disutility from supplying labor in polluted environments, which can induce earlier retirement even when health and income remain stable. The model shows that abatement systematically postpones retirement by reducing exposure, whereas health effort produces a more nuanced effect—it can either extend or shorten working life depending on how it interacts with environmental decisions. This finding qualifies the common view that health investment unambiguously delays retirement, highlighting instead that the relationship depends critically on environmental conditions. Calibration to U.S. data illustrates the model’s quantitative implications: consumption and abatement follow opposite U-shaped patterns over the life cycle, health expenditure rises steeply with age, and the value of environmental quality declines toward retirement. These insights are general and offer a new lens on how pollution, health investment, and retirement interact.

### A Matter of Timing: Health and Economic Responses to Pandemic Shocks over the Life Cycle

**Status:** Under review at the *Journal of Health Economics*

**Abstract:** We develop an analytical and quantitative life-cycle model in which individuals respond to pandemic shocks by adjusting health expenditure, consumption, and labor supply. Pandemics raise mortality risk, increasing the value of survival and health spending, but simultaneously worsen labor-market conditions, creating opposing pressures on individuals' work and retirement decisions. We show analytically that the health-driven and labor-driven responses are governed by the pandemic's timing over the life cycle, and derive conditions under which health-expenditure responses dominate consumption and retirement responses. A calibrated model shows that pandemics near retirement generate large increases in health expenditure and welfare losses, driven primarily by elevated mortality risk, while early-life pandemics leave health spending largely unaffected and instead alter consumption and saving.

### Occupational Variation in Work Strain: A Life-Cycle Model of Retirement

**Abstract:** We extend a dynamic life-cycle model of endogenous retirement in which individuals choose consumption, health expenditure, and the timing of retirement to incorporate occupational heterogeneity through differential work strain, which raises the disutility of labor effort in occupation-specific ways. Health investments influence survival probabilities, while the retirement decision balances the utility from continued labor income against the rising disutility of work and the value of leisure. We derive analytical conditions characterizing optimal consumption, health expenditure, and retirement, and examine how occupational vulnerability to working conditions shapes retirement age and welfare. The framework highlights the interaction of health, survival, and retirement in the presence of climate-induced labor disutility, offering implications for welfare analysis and adaptation policy.
